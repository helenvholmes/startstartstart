Rebuilding night-shift handover

Cutting a 40-minute paper ritual to ten, for 1,200 nurses across three hospitals.

My role
Lead product designer
Team
1 PM, 4 engineers, 2 nurse advisors
Timeline
7 months, research to rollout
Outcome
Handover down from 40 to 10 minutes

The problem

At every shift change, the outgoing nurse walks the incoming one through each patient: medications due, tests pending, anything that changed overnight. On the wards we worked with, that happened on paper. It took around 40 minutes a shift, and details slipped through when a sheet was out of date or someone's handwriting was hard to read.

An earlier attempt had put the paper form on a tablet, field for field. Nurses stopped using it within a month.

What I learned

I sat in on 14 handovers, day and night, and interviewed 22 nurses. The form wasn't the problem. The order was: nurses don't think about patients bed by bed, they think about who needs attention first.

“I don't need everything about every patient. I need to know who's going to need me in the next hour.”Charge nurse, surgical ward
  • The most important information was what had changed, not the full history.
  • Handover happened standing up, in corridors, often one-handed.
  • Nurses trusted information they'd written themselves more than anything auto-filled.

Designing it

My first version was a smarter checklist. In testing it was faster, but nurses still read every field aloud, so most of the time came back. The breakthrough was dropping the form entirely: each patient became a card, sorted by urgency, showing only what had changed since the last shift. Everything else was one tap away.

We tested five rounds of prototypes on the wards, on real shifts, with the nurse advisors deciding what counted as urgent. I kept a "write it yourself" note on every card, because the research showed nurses wouldn't trust a summary they hadn't touched.

Results

10 minaverage handover, down from 40
94%of nurses using it daily after three months
30%fewer missed-medication reports

It's now the standard handover at all three hospitals, and two more are piloting it.

What I'd do differently

I'd bring the night shift in earlier. Our first two rounds of research happened mostly during the day, and night-shift nurses, who do a third of all handovers, had needs we only found late: fewer people around to ask, and a much stronger need to see what the day team had already tried.
